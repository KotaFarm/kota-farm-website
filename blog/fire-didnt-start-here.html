The Fire Didn't Start Here

What a neighbour's dry field, a single spark, and a changing climate taught us about farm design.

Climate April 20, 2026 · 5 min read

Before This Is a Story About Fire

Before this is a story about fire, it is a story about neighbours. A farm does not live alone. What the farm next to yours does — what they spray, what they flood, what they leave behind — can change the fate of your soil, your water, and your crop. Over time we have come to see three ways a neighbouring farm can reach across the fence line and affect ours:

Three ways a neighbour's farm can change yours

  • Chemicals. Pesticide and herbicide drift from conventional spraying — carried by wind, it settles on your leaves, your soil, and eventually your food.
  • Flooding. Heavy water use on neighbouring paddy (rice) fields — over-irrigation seeps sideways and raises the water table under your own land, creating unwanted moisture, waterlogged roots, and salt build-up.
  • Fire. Dry fields left empty between crops, catching on any spark and racing into the next farm, and the next.

This blog is about the third. It happened to us yesterday.

A Wall of Flame Across the Next Field

On the afternoon of April 19, 2026, smoke rose over Khedli Pandya. Not from our farm — from the next one. Within minutes, a wall of flame was moving across the wheat stubble of the neighbouring field, pulled forward by the dry pre-monsoon wind. By the time anyone reached the boundary, hundreds of acres of surrounding farmland were on fire.

Our farm caught the edge of it. We lost 10 plants — two years of growth — and the small bamboo-thatched hut that housed our tubewell. But the real story isn't what we lost. It's why this keeps happening, and why it will likely happen again.

What Is Parali, and Why Does It Burn?

Across the fields that surround us, farmers grow wheat in the winter season. By early April, the harvest is done. What's left behind is parali — the dry stalks and stubble of the cut crop, rooted in earth that has already started to bake under the rising summer sun.

Parali is extraordinarily flammable. After a Rajasthan winter and the first weeks of April heat, it is bone-dry. Traditionally, many farmers burn it on purpose — a quick, cheap way to clear the field and prepare it for the next crop. This year, the farmers near us didn't light it. They knew the wind was wrong, the heat was rising, the risk was high. And it still caught fire anyway.

A spark — from a wire, a cigarette, a passing tractor — is all it takes. Once a dry parali field catches, the fire moves at the speed of the wind.

What Reached Us

The fire crossed our north boundary and stopped, mostly, at the edge of the green. Ten plants — two years old, just starting to give us shade and roots — were burned. That hurt, but the real damage was infrastructure.

Our tubewell sits near the boundary. We had housed the motor and control system inside a bamboo-and-thatch hut — a deliberate sustainability choice, using farm-grown materials instead of concrete and steel. The hut burned completely. The drip irrigation network feeding the nearby beds is gone. The motor itself slipped down into the well shaft as the structure collapsed. We are now bringing in a drilling rig to pull it back out.

Before. Bamboo, thatch, and drip lines — the tubewell hut, with our pond just behind.
After. The brick pillars are all that's left — and a blue bucket in the corner, which was the first line of defence.

Why the Rest of the Farm Held

The 10 plants we lost sit on the immediate boundary. Everything behind them — the food forest, the vegetable beds, the mature trees — did not burn. That wasn't luck. It was design.

Our fields were green. We had standing crops, living mulch, and canopy trees holding moisture in the soil and the air. We have a pond just inside the northern fence line, which acted as a natural firebreak: the fire reached the water and stopped. A monoculture field is a desert between seasons — dry, dead, and combustible. A permaculture farm is never fully empty. That difference is what kept the rest of the farm standing.

The line between what burned and what didn't. Scorched parali on the left, our green farm on the right. The drill rig has come to pull the motor back out.

This Is the Second Year

This is not the first time. The same thing happened last year, on a neighbouring farm boundary, in the same season. Two years running — which means this isn't an accident. It is a pattern.

The state recognises the problem but has no real answer for it. Across Kota district, the government's response is to cut off farm electricity from 9 AM to 6 PM for the entire summer, to reduce the chance of accidental short-circuit ignitions on dry fields. When the official fire-prevention policy is "turn off the power," you know the system has run out of ideas.

Meanwhile, summers keep getting hotter. April in Kota now routinely crosses 42°C. Soil holds less moisture. Stubble dries faster. Every year, the window between "safe" and "catastrophic" narrows.

What We're Asking Our Neighbours

You can't control what the farm next to you does. But of the three pressures above, fire is the one where a simple, profitable change on a neighbour's field would protect everyone on the road. So this is what we are asking.

A simple ask to every farmer on the road

  • Don't leave the field empty in the crop-changing season. Even a short, simple cover crop closes the window of fire risk.
  • Plant nitrogen-fixing legumes between wheat and the next crop — dal (lentils), chana (chickpea), moong. They fix nitrogen into the soil for free, don't need much water, give a small harvest, and keep the field alive and green.
  • Don't burn parali to clear fields. Mulch it back into the soil, compost it, or let animals graze it down. Every acre of burned stubble is a potential source for the next wildfire.
  • Talk to your neighbours. The economics work — legumes earn money and improve the next crop. The fire risk affects everyone on the road.

And What Every Farm Can Do for Itself

If you cannot be sure what your neighbour's field will look like in April, design for the worst case:

Farm-level fire resilience

  • A water body near your boundary. A pond, a large tank, even a wide check dam. It is a firebreak that doubles as irrigation storage.
  • Trees along the fence line. A living green wall holds moisture and slows wind-driven fire. Choose species that stay green into summer.
  • Green ground cover year-round. Mulched, alive, shaded soil does not burn easily. Bare dry soil does.
  • Keep critical infrastructure away from the edge. Our tubewell was near the boundary for convenience. We're rebuilding it further in.
  • Diversify. Monoculture leaves fields empty between seasons. A food forest is never empty.

What We're Doing Next

The drill rig is on site to pull the motor out of the well. Over the next few weeks we'll rebuild the irrigation system — further from the boundary this time. We're adding a proper firebreak along the north edge, and we're starting conversations with farmers on the surrounding roads about parali and cover crops.

A fire that doesn't start on your farm can still take parts of your farm. The answer isn't a bigger fence. It's better soil — on our land, and on the land around us. We'll keep doing our part, and keep asking others to do theirs.
